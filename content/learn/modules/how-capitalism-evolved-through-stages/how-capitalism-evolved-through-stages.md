## Why the stages matter

The word "capitalism" is used as if it describes a single, stable system. In practice, the institutional arrangements that organise production, investment, and distribution have changed so radically over five centuries that a cotton mill owner in 1820 and an algorithmic trading desk in 2024 are operating inside the same nominal category but under almost entirely different logics.

Understanding the stages matters for two reasons. First, it makes visible the political choices that produced each stage — and therefore the political choices that could produce the next one. Second, it allows specific critiques: not "capitalism is bad" but "this specific feature of shareholder capitalism — the decoupling of productivity from wages — was introduced at a specific moment for specific reasons and can be redesigned."

The historian Karl Polanyi's insight remains useful: the economy is always embedded in social and political institutions, even when dominant ideology insists otherwise. The "free market" is never free of politics — it is constituted by political choices about property rights, contract law, money creation, and the enforcement of claims.

## Stage 1: Merchant capitalism (roughly 1500–1800)

The first recognisably capitalist institutions were not factories but trading companies — the British East India Company (1600), the Dutch VOC (1602), and their predecessors. These entities combined state-granted monopoly power, joint-stock ownership that spread risk across investors, and the capacity to use violence to enforce commercial claims.

Profit in the merchant stage came not from producing things but from trading them — specifically from the price difference between what goods cost in one market and what they could command in another. The slave trade was not a marginal aberration of this system; it was one of its most profitable subsystems. The triangular trade between Europe, West Africa, and the Americas generated the capital accumulation that helped fund the industrial revolution.

**What the state did:** Granted monopoly charters, provided naval protection, maintained colonial legal systems, and suppressed competition by other European powers. The line between state power and corporate power was not a line at all — it was a partnership.

> The East India Company was not "too big to fail" — it was too fused with the state to be allowed to fail. The British government bailed it out in 1773 and eventually nationalised its governance functions in 1858. The template of state-underwritten private profit is older than most people assume.

## Stage 2: Industrial capitalism (1800–1914)

Steam power and the factory system shifted the primary source of profit from trade to production. The crucial move was concentrating workers in factories where their labour could be organised, measured, and extracted at scale. Wages were set at or near subsistence (what workers needed to survive and reproduce the labour force), while the gap between wages and the value workers produced — what Marx called surplus value — accrued to capital.

The industrial stage produced capitalism's characteristic social form: the proletariat — people who owned no productive property and therefore had to sell their labour as a commodity. It also produced, inevitably, the first organised resistance: trade unions, the Chartist movement for democratic rights, the First and Second Internationals, and the early socialist parties.

It is worth noting that industrial capitalism did not produce rising living standards automatically or quickly. Real wages in Britain stagnated or fell for decades during the industrial revolution's early phase. The gains came later — and largely because workers organised to demand them.

## Stage 3: Finance capitalism (1890–1940)

As industrial firms grew larger, they required more capital than any individual entrepreneur or family could supply. Banks and investment houses stepped in — and in doing so, gained effective control over which industries expanded, on what terms, and under whose management.

Rudolf Hilferding, writing in 1910, described "finance capital" as the fusion of bank capital with industrial capital under financial control. J.P. Morgan's restructuring of US Steel in 1901 — effectively merging Carnegie Steel with a dozen competitors to create the first US company worth more than $1 billion — is the classic illustration. Morgan did not build anything; he reorganised the ownership and financing of what already existed, and captured enormous value in the process.

Lenin drew on Hilferding's analysis to argue that imperialism — the division of the world into colonial spheres — was the inevitable outgrowth of finance capitalism. Excess capital in the industrial centres needed new markets and new sources of raw material and cheap labour; colonial control provided them.

The finance capitalism stage ended in the Depression — partly because financial instability (including overleveraged speculation in stock markets) destroyed demand, partly because the political backlash against financial elites was severe enough to produce New Deal regulation and, in Europe, fascism and social democracy as competing responses.

## Stage 4: Keynesian managed capitalism (1945–1975)

The post-war settlement was not an accident. It was the outcome of specific political conditions: organised labour with genuine bargaining power, elites chastened by the Depression and the war, Cold War competition that made social concessions strategically necessary, and a theoretical framework — Keynesian economics — that justified active state management of demand.

The Bretton Woods agreement (1944) created a managed international monetary order: fixed exchange rates, capital controls, and the IMF and World Bank as institutions of international economic governance. Within this framework, national governments pursued active fiscal and monetary policies aimed at full employment. Welfare states expanded across Western Europe. Collective bargaining reached 30–60% of workers in most OECD countries.

The results were striking by almost any metric. GDP per capita in Western Europe grew faster between 1950 and 1973 than in any comparable period before or since. The labour share of income rose. The Gini coefficient fell across most OECD countries. Life expectancy rose substantially. Child poverty fell.

**Why it ended:** The system faced a genuine problem in the 1970s — stagflation, the simultaneous occurrence of high inflation and high unemployment, which Keynesian models had not adequately predicted or provided tools to address. But the response was a political choice, not an economic inevitability. The choice to use high interest rates (Volcker shock, 1979–82) and to break the labour movement's bargaining power rather than to redesign the Keynesian framework was made by specific people with specific interests.

## Stage 5: Shareholder capitalism (1976–present)

The shareholder capitalism stage was inaugurated by two documents and a political decision. The first document was Milton Friedman's 1970 New York Times essay arguing that "the social responsibility of business is to increase its profits." The second was the Powell Memo (1971), a strategic blueprint by corporate lawyer Lewis Powell urging business to mobilise politically to roll back regulation, fund business-friendly academic research, and reshape legal and economic orthodoxy.

The political decision was the Federal Reserve's Volcker shock in 1979–82: raising interest rates to 20% to break inflation, triggering a severe recession that broke union bargaining power (Reagan's firing of the PATCO air traffic controllers in 1981 was the symbolic moment) and reoriented corporate strategy toward financial returns.

The institutional consequences were specific and measurable:

- **Share buybacks** replaced investment in productive capacity as the primary use of corporate profit. S&P 500 companies spent $6 trillion on buybacks between 2010 and 2020.
- **Wages decoupled from productivity.** US productivity rose 65% between 1979 and 2020; median wages rose 16%.
- **Executive compensation** shifted from salary to stock options, directly aligning CEO incentives with share price rather than long-term firm health.
- **Financial sector profit** grew from roughly 10% of US corporate profits in 1950 to 40% by 2001.
- **Risk transfer:** Defined benefit pensions became defined contribution plans (401k); secure employment became flexible contracts and eventually gig arrangements.

> The Business Roundtable's 2019 statement abandoning shareholder primacy in favour of "stakeholder capitalism" was signed by 181 CEOs. A study published the following year found no measurable difference in their firms' behaviour — executive compensation packages remained tied to share price, and no governance changes were implemented to give other stakeholders structural power.

## Stage 6: Platform and surveillance capitalism (2000s–present)

Shoshana Zuboff's concept of "surveillance capitalism," developed in her 2019 book of the same name, identifies a qualitative shift within and beyond the shareholder stage. The raw material of the platform economy is not goods, not labour in the traditional sense, but behavioural data — the residue of human experience harvested at scale from digital interactions.

Google's insight, developed around 2000, was that search query data could be used to build prediction models about which ads a given user was likely to click — and that these predictions could be sold. This is not advertising in the traditional sense. It is a prediction product derived from a raw material (behavioural data) that users did not know they were supplying and for which they receive no compensation.

The logic generalised rapidly. Facebook built the same infrastructure around social behaviour. Amazon applied it to purchasing behaviour. The advertising market restructured around predicted behaviour rather than mass reach. And crucially, the platforms discovered that their prediction products improved if they could also shape behaviour — so the systems were designed not just to predict what users would do, but to nudge them toward actions that increased ad revenue.

**What makes this a distinct stage:**

1. **The raw material is behavioural data**, not physical inputs or labour time in the traditional sense.
2. **Users are not the customer** — they are the source of raw material. The customer is whoever buys prediction products.
3. **Network effects** create winner-take-most markets in which the largest platform compounds its advantage.
4. **The product modifies behaviour**, not just predicts it — platforms have economic incentives to shape what users do, not just observe it.

## Stakeholder capitalism: new stage or rebranding?

Klaus Schwab and the World Economic Forum have promoted "stakeholder capitalism" — the idea that corporations should serve employees, communities, and the environment alongside shareholders — since 1971. It has received renewed attention since 2019.

The structural question is: what enforcement mechanism ensures corporations actually prioritise stakeholders when doing so conflicts with share price? The Business Roundtable statement and ESG commitments rely on voluntary executive discretion. History suggests this is not sufficient: when shareholder returns and stakeholder interests conflict without legal or structural constraint, shareholders win.

Genuine stakeholder capitalism would require structural changes: board representation for workers (as in German codetermination), enforceable environmental obligations, legal changes to corporate purpose, and executive compensation decoupled from share price. Some of these exist in some jurisdictions. The label "stakeholder capitalism" often describes aspiration without mechanism.

## What a next stage might require

Historical stage transitions share a pattern: a crisis exposes the contradictions of the current stage, creates political space for new institutional arrangements, and a settlement is reached through political conflict. The settlement is not determined in advance — it reflects the relative power of competing interests at the moment of maximum disruption.

The contradictions currently accumulating:

- **Shareholder capitalism** has produced wage stagnation, supply chain fragility, and a political backlash that has not yet settled into a new institutional form.
- **Platform capitalism** has produced market concentration, behavioural manipulation at scale, and a regulatory response that lags by a decade.
- **Climate crisis** imposes a physical constraint that neither current stage has a coherent mechanism to address.

These contradictions do not automatically produce a better stage. The 1930s crisis produced both the New Deal and fascism. The design of the next institutional settlement is an open political question.
