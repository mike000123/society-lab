## The diagnosis

The Democracy & Better Governance path traced democracy's structural weaknesses across eight modules. Electoral rules designed for two-party systems misrepresent pluralist electorates. Lobbying converts economic power into political power faster than electoral accountability can compensate. Corruption creates a parallel system of private governance that captures public institutions. The electoral cycle is structurally mismatched with the timescales of the problems democracies most need to solve. And the design of specific institutions — the EU's legislative complexity, the US's veto points — shapes what is politically possible within each system.

The movement history modules added a crucial insight: these structural weaknesses are not permanent. The franchise was extended under sustained pressure. Electoral systems have been reformed. Anti-corruption institutions have been built. The question is not whether change is possible but what conditions make it happen.

## The reform agenda

**Structural electoral reform** changes what kinds of politics are rational. Proportional representation and ranked-choice voting reduce the winner-take-all dynamic that rewards polarisation and punishes nuance. New Zealand's experience with MMP shows that electoral reform produces more representative parliaments, more coalition-building, and more durable policy. Alaska's ranked-choice voting reduced the advantage of extreme candidates. These are not radical experiments — they are well-tested alternatives to first-past-the-post.

**Anti-corruption infrastructure** creates the conditions under which other reforms become possible. Independent anti-corruption agencies with prosecutorial authority, mandatory lobbying disclosure, and revolving-door rules all raise the cost of political capture. Hong Kong's ICAC and Latvia's KNAB both transformed highly corrupt systems within a decade. The institutional design matters: agencies need insulation from political interference, prosecutorial authority, and adequate resources.

**Long-horizon institutions** address the democratic deficit on future-oriented problems. Citizens' assemblies — randomly selected, deliberating over months with expert input — have produced actionable recommendations on abortion, marriage equality, and climate that legislatures could not produce through normal politics. Ireland's use of citizens' assemblies to break two constitutional deadlocks shows the mechanism working at national scale. Future Generations Commissioners and parliamentary futures committees create a form of institutional representation for people not yet born.

**Public campaign finance** breaks the structural link between wealth and political access. State funding of parties and candidates, matched small-donor programmes, and spending caps all reduce politicians' dependence on large donors — the necessary precondition for anti-corruption and anti-lobbying reform to be politically viable.

## Sequence matters

Electoral reform tends to unlock further reform by changing who enters politics and what is politically rational for them. Anti-corruption enforcement builds the institutional legitimacy that makes more ambitious change possible. Citizens' assemblies generate mandates for specific changes that partisan legislatures lack. Starting with the most legible reforms — lobbying transparency, independent anti-corruption bodies — creates the foundation for structural change.
