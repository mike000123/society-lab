## The basic arithmetic of the carbon budget

The IPCC's Sixth Assessment Report estimates that a remaining carbon budget of approximately 400 GtCO₂ gives a 67% chance of limiting warming to 1.5°C. Global CO₂ emissions from energy and industry run at roughly 37–38 GtCO₂ per year. At that rate, the budget is exhausted in around ten years.

This arithmetic creates a specific challenge for economic growth. If the global economy grows at 3% per year — below the average of recent decades — and energy intensity (emissions per unit of GDP) improves at 2% per year (roughly the historical rate), total emissions still rise at approximately 1% per year. Growth outpaces efficiency gains.

Staying within the budget while maintaining GDP growth requires not just efficiency improvement but absolute replacement of fossil energy with zero-carbon alternatives — and at a pace fast enough to absorb new energy demand created by growth itself. The numbers are not impossible, but they are far more demanding than public optimism typically acknowledges.

> The critical distinction: relative decoupling means emissions per unit of GDP fall. Absolute decoupling means total emissions fall. Most rich-country progress is relative. The carbon budget requires absolute.

## The Jevons Paradox

William Stanley Jevons observed in 1865 that James Watt's improved steam engine, far from reducing coal consumption in England, caused it to increase dramatically. The engine's efficiency made coal-powered production profitable in many more applications, at much larger scale. Total coal use exploded.

The same dynamic recurs across energy systems. LED lighting is roughly 75% more efficient than incandescent bulbs. When LEDs became cheap after 2010, electricity use for lighting was expected to fall sharply. Instead, global electricity consumption for lighting increased — because low cost made lighting viable in new contexts (streets, advertising, commercial spaces) and expanded access in developing economies.

Fuel-efficient cars enable longer commutes. Energy-efficient industrial processes lower production costs, enabling more production. Data centres get more efficient per computation, but the number of computations grows faster. This is the rebound effect — and at full scale, where efficiency gains are reinvested in growth rather than reduction, it is called the Jevons Paradox.

The implication is not that efficiency is useless — it is essential. But efficiency alone, in a growing economy with no constraint on total throughput, does not guarantee lower total emissions. Something else has to change.

## The outsourcing problem in production-based accounting

The standard measure of national emissions counts what is produced within a country's borders. This creates a systematic blind spot for economies that deindustrialise and import what they previously manufactured.

The UK's production-based emissions fell roughly 44% between 1990 and 2019, a figure frequently cited as evidence of successful decoupling. But the UK also shifted much of its manufacturing to Asia over this period. On a consumption basis — counting emissions embedded in imported goods alongside domestic production — the reduction was approximately 15%.

The gap between these two numbers represents carbon that was outsourced rather than eliminated. Similar patterns hold for Germany, France, and the United States: consumption-based footprints are materially higher than production-based figures, and a significant portion of apparent decoupling reflects where goods are made rather than how they are made.

This matters for how the transition is understood. Countries that believe they have already solved the problem through efficiency and deindustrialisation may underinvest in the structural changes needed in consumption, land use, and the hard-to-abate sectors.

## Hard-to-abate sectors

Roughly 30% of global emissions come from sectors where no scalable low-carbon technology currently exists at competitive cost: cement (CO₂ is a chemical byproduct of the production process, not just combustion), steel (requires high-temperature reduction reactions currently dependent on coke), aviation (battery energy density is orders of magnitude below jet fuel), shipping (similar), and agriculture (methane from livestock, nitrous oxide from fertilisers).

These sectors are expected to grow as the global middle class expands. Cement production is closely tied to urbanisation and infrastructure investment in lower-income countries. Demand for air travel has historically risen with income. Together, the hard-to-abate sectors represent the portion of the decoupling challenge for which there is currently no technical answer operating at meaningful scale.
