## Big picture
It is common to describe corruption as theft, but "hidden tax" is often the better teaching phrase. A corrupt system makes people pay extra just to access things that should already be part of their rights or the normal functioning of government. The payment might be literal cash, a favor, a connection, an avoidable delay, or the need to keep trying until the right gatekeeper says yes.

That is why corruption is not only a moral problem. It is also a systems-efficiency problem. It raises transaction costs, distorts who gets what, and teaches citizens that the official rulebook is less important than private access.

> Once people expect informal tollbooths inside formal institutions, trust and productivity both start to leak away.

The attached evidence pages help locate corruption inside a wider governance picture.

## Why the system reproduces itself
Corruption is sticky because those who benefit from it also gain resources and networks that help protect it. Honest actors then adapt defensively. Firms spend on access instead of innovation. Citizens look for patrons instead of rules. Officials may underperform because professional norms have already been hollowed out.

This is why anti-corruption work cannot rely only on slogans. It usually requires reducing discretionary chokepoints, increasing transparency, protecting investigators, improving administrative process, and making honest service more viable than extractive service.

## What a learner should focus on
The key lesson is not that every weak service outcome comes from corruption. It is that corruption changes incentives across the whole field. It makes fair access feel uncertain. It pushes energy toward connections. And it leaves the system looking more expensive and less capable than the official budget alone would suggest.

In other words, corruption is not a side defect. It changes how the entire machine behaves.
