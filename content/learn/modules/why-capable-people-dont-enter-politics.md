## Why elections select for different traits than governance requires

Democratic elections are competitive performances. Candidates must raise money, speak well in public, manage media narratives, project confidence and decisiveness, and endure sustained personal attack without visible damage. These are real skills, but they are not the same skills as managing large organisations, building coalitions of experts around complex policy problems, or thinking clearly about second-order consequences.

Plato's critique in *The Republic* — that democratic systems select for demagogues rather than philosopher-kings — is too strong. But the kernel of the observation is enduring: the traits that help you get elected and the traits that help you govern well are different, and selecting for the former does not guarantee the latter.

Research on political selection supports this. Studies of legislatures in multiple countries find that politicians are drawn disproportionately from certain occupational backgrounds (law, business, local government) and that this is partly a selection artifact of who can sustain a campaign rather than who is most competent at governance. A 2018 paper by Dal Bó et al. using Swedish registry data found that politicians are taller and more intelligent on average than the general population, but less intelligent than comparably paid professionals in other fields — suggesting that higher-ability people are sorting away from politics.

## The rising cost of candidacy

In most democracies, the personal cost of running for office has risen substantially. In the US, the median winning Senate candidate now raises tens of millions of dollars. This means the candidate must either be personally wealthy, willing to spend large amounts of time soliciting wealthy donors, or both. The pool of people willing and able to do this is narrower than the pool of people who could govern well.

The psychological cost has also increased. Continuous media scrutiny, social media attack, and the permanent availability of any past statement or action creates an environment where the downside risk of candidacy has grown. Researchers studying candidate emergence consistently find that people with high-quality private-sector options — senior professionals, executives, academics — are particularly deterred by this asymmetry: the expected upside of winning is modest compared to their private-sector alternatives; the downside of losing, or of governing under constant personal attack, is severe.

> The result is a two-stage filter: first, the very best people don't run; second, the people who do run are selected for traits suited to campaigning rather than governing.

## How polarisation compounds the problem

In systems with primary elections, candidates must first win a primary election before facing the general electorate. Primary electorates are typically smaller and more ideologically extreme than general electorates. This creates pressure toward positions that are further from the centre — positions that may appeal to primary voters but that reduce governing flexibility.

Several political scientists have argued that this creates a competence-loyalty tradeoff: candidates who demonstrate ideological commitment to primary voters are rewarded with the nomination, but the same commitment constrains their ability to make pragmatic policy compromises once in office. Comparative research finds that countries without primary systems — where party leaders select candidates based partly on competence criteria — tend to have higher legislative quality on average, though this comes with its own accountability costs.

## What other democracies do differently

The gap between who runs and who could govern well is not the same everywhere. Several institutional designs partially mitigate the problem. Proportional representation systems tend to produce more diverse candidate pools and reduce the premium on individual performance skills. Parliamentary systems that draw cabinet members from within the legislature allow parties to cultivate governing talent over time. Many democracies have established independent civil service traditions that ensure expert capacity exists regardless of who wins elections.

None of these are complete solutions — they carry their own failure modes — but they illustrate that the structure of how candidates are selected and officials are chosen is not fixed. It is a design choice with predictable consequences.
