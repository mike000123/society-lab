## Big picture
Doughnut economics starts with a correction that sounds obvious once it is said clearly: the economy is not floating above nature. It is nested inside society, and society is nested inside the biosphere. That means the real question is not how to maximize growth in the abstract. It is how to meet human needs without breaking the living systems that make those needs possible to meet in the first place.

The doughnut image gives this idea a simple shape. The inner ring is the social foundation: food, health, energy, housing, education, care, political voice, and basic material security. The outer ring is the ecological ceiling: climate stability, functioning ecosystems, freshwater limits, land pressure, pollution loads, and other boundaries that cannot be treated as infinite sinks.

> The teaching move here is powerful because it changes the target. The goal is no longer "grow first, repair later." The goal becomes "design an economy that is socially sufficient and ecologically survivable at the same time."

## What the evidence layer shows
The three attached evidence pages work well together because they show different sides of the same constraint.

- Emissions data shows who has contributed most in total, who emits most per person, and why responsibility looks different depending on whether you focus on current flows or cumulative buildup.
- Energy data shows that the transition is not only about moral preference. It is about the physical systems that power homes, transport, industry, and heat.
- Biodiversity data shows why climate is only one part of ecological overshoot. A society can reduce one pressure and still be undermining the ecosystems it depends on through habitat loss, extraction, and land-use change.

These visuals help anchor the doughnut idea in physical systems. A society cannot claim to be “inside limits” if its material metabolism still depends on steadily rising fossil throughput or if its climate burden keeps climbing.

## Why this is also a financial story
One of the most important lessons for Society Lab is that ecological economics is not only about environmental policy. It is also about money, credit, and investment direction.

If private finance naturally flows toward existing assets, high short-run returns, and familiar infrastructure, then the economy keeps reproducing the old pattern. Retrofit, resilient grids, public transport, restoration, and energy efficiency often need standards, public banking, guarantees, industrial policy, or other steering mechanisms because the default system does not move capital there fast enough on its own.

## What a learner should take away
The doughnut is useful because it makes trade-offs visible instead of hiding them. A country can improve some green indicators while leaving people below the social floor. It can also reduce deprivation in the short term through forms of growth that deepen ecological overshoot. Good policy has to care about both failures at once.

That is why the green economy cannot be judged only by investment headlines or carbon intensity averages. The deeper question is whether the system is changing what it builds, who benefits, and whether the material basis of life is becoming more secure rather than more brittle.
