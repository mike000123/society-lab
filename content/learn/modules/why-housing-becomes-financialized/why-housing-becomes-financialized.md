## From shelter to asset class

Housing has always been expensive relative to income. But the specific version of the problem most cities face today — where median house prices are 10, 12, or 15 times median annual wages — is relatively recent. It accelerated sharply after financial deregulation in the 1980s and 1990s made mortgage credit much cheaper and more widely available.

More credit chasing a relatively fixed supply of desirable urban land pushes prices up. Rising prices make housing more attractive as an investment — not just for large institutions but for ordinary households, who are encouraged by tax policy and financial advisors to treat property as their primary savings vehicle. More buyers treating housing as an investment further drives up prices. The feedback loop is self-reinforcing.

This is the financialisation of housing: housing markets becoming dominated not just by the logic of shelter (I need somewhere to live) but by the logic of asset accumulation (this property should appreciate faster than my savings account).

> Once housing becomes the primary wealth-building vehicle for the middle class, reforming it threatens existing homeowners — which is most of the voting population.

## What financialisation looks like in practice

In the United Kingdom, house prices rose roughly 500% in real terms between 1970 and 2020, while real wages roughly doubled. The average house now costs around 8–9 times average earnings nationally; in London it exceeds 12 times. This is not explained by construction costs or raw material prices. It reflects the cumulative effect of cheap mortgage credit, constrained planning, and decades of treating housing as an investment vehicle.

In Canada, Australia, and New Zealand — economies with similar financial structures — the pattern is identical. House price-to-income ratios that were around 3–4 in the 1980s are now 7–10 in major cities. The common factors across all these cases are not geography or culture. They are mortgage credit expansion and policies that made owner-occupied housing tax-advantaged.

In Germany, by contrast, stricter tenancy protections, lower homeownership rates, and a tradition of renting as a long-term stable option produced far smaller real house price increases over the same period. The comparison illustrates that these outcomes are policy choices, not market inevitabilities.

## Who benefits and who pays

Rising house prices are not neutral. They transfer wealth from people who do not own property to people who do. They transfer wealth from younger generations to older ones. They transfer wealth from renters — who see their costs rise with asset values — to landlords, whose income rises.

When a country's retirement system and middle-class wealth are built around rising property values, reforming housing policy becomes politically very difficult. Existing homeowners — typically the majority of voters in older democracies — have a structural interest in maintaining scarcity. Planning restrictions, limits on new housing types, and neighbourhood character protections often reflect this interest more than they reflect design or environmental values.

This creates a political economy where the people with the most power to change housing policy are precisely the people who benefit from not changing it.

## What would change the trajectory

Effective reform generally requires working on several fronts simultaneously. Loosening planning rules expands supply but does not prevent the new supply from being absorbed by investors if credit remains unlimited. Taxing land value or capital gains more aggressively reduces the investment premium but faces intense political opposition from existing owners. Social and public housing at scale shifts a portion of supply permanently out of the investment market.

The historical examples of more affordable housing — Vienna's municipal housing, Singapore's public housing covering over 80% of residents, Germany's stable tenancy market — all share a common feature: the state treated housing as infrastructure, not as an asset whose price growth should be encouraged.
