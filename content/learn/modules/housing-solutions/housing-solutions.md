## The housing crisis is a system failure

The Housing & Affordability path traced the crisis to its structural roots. Banks create money by issuing mortgages — mortgage credit and house prices rise together in a self-reinforcing loop. Land near jobs and amenities is scarce and impossible to replicate; whoever holds it captures the value created by everyone else. Zoning laws designed around car ownership and homeowner preferences prevent the density that would ease pressure. And wealth compounding faster than wages means asset-holders pull further ahead of renters with every cycle.

These causes interlock. More mortgage credit inflates prices. Inflated prices make land a better investment than productive activity. Restrictive zoning limits supply. Limited supply justifies more credit. The system reproduces unaffordability even when individual actors behave rationally within it.

## The reform agenda

Three interlocking changes address the different layers of the problem.

**Decommodify land.** Community land trusts permanently remove land from speculative markets by holding it in perpetual trust — selling only the structure, not the ground. Vienna's century-old social housing programme and Singapore's Housing Development Board show that public and social housing, properly funded and managed, acts as a rent anchor for entire cities, not just a safety net. Vacancy taxes on idle land force productive use and recover some of the publicly created value that landowners currently capture.

**Reform planning and finance.** Zoning reform that allows density near transit and jobs increases supply without sprawl. Land value taxation shifts the tax burden from buildings to land, removing the incentive to hold urban land idle. Capping mortgage credit growth — or requiring higher deposits during boom cycles — limits the credit-price spiral that makes each recovery more expensive than the last.

**Redistribute the gains.** The wealth that compounds through property ownership is partly publicly created — through infrastructure, schools, and planning decisions. Progressive land value capture, infrastructure levies, and inheritance reform redirect some of that gain toward the people whose labour and taxes created it.

## What has actually worked

Vienna and Singapore are the two most-cited examples of large-scale success — both achieved through sustained public commitment over decades, not single-policy fixes. Burlington, Vermont's community land trust has preserved affordable homeownership through three housing booms. Vancouver's empty homes tax reduced vacancy rates. None of these are perfect; all of them demonstrate that political will, not technical impossibility, is the binding constraint.

## The political economy

Housing reform faces a structural obstacle: existing homeowners benefit from rising prices and are disproportionately represented among voters and legislators. Reform coalitions that work include renters, younger voters, business groups concerned about labour supply, and social housing advocates — a broader coalition than housing advocates alone.

The window for reform typically opens during affordability crises acute enough to reach voters who previously benefited from rising prices. That window is currently open in most wealthy countries.
