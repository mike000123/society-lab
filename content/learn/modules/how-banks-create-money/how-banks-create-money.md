## The mechanism most textbooks skip

The traditional story of banking goes like this: people deposit savings, banks pool those savings and lend a fraction out, keeping reserves for withdrawals. This is the "fractional reserve" model taught in most introductory economics courses. It is largely wrong in its description of how modern banking actually works.

The Bank of England stated it clearly in a 2014 paper: "Commercial banks create money, in the form of bank deposits, by making new loans. When a bank makes a loan, for example to someone taking out a mortgage to buy a house, it does not typically do so by giving them thousands of pounds worth of existing deposits. Instead, it credits their bank account with a deposit of the size of the mortgage."

In other words, the bank does not find the money first and then lend it. The act of lending **creates** the deposit. The money comes into existence at the moment the loan is approved. When the loan is repaid, that money is destroyed. The money supply expands and contracts with the aggregate lending decisions of commercial banks.

> Banks do not intermediate money. They manufacture it — as a byproduct of lending at interest.

## What this means for where money goes

If commercial banks create money through lending, then where they choose to lend determines what the new money finances. Banks lend against collateral and to creditworthy borrowers. This has a structural bias: lending against existing assets — houses, financial instruments, other securities — is safer and more profitable than financing new productive activity.

The practical result, visible across most developed economies since the 1980s, is that a growing share of bank credit has gone into property and financial asset purchases rather than into business investment, wages, or new productive capacity. This is not a conspiracy — it is an incentive structure. Property prices rising predictably over time, combined with hard collateral, makes mortgage lending far more attractive than backing a new manufacturing plant.

Bank of England data for the UK shows that in 1986, roughly 25% of bank lending went to residential mortgages. By 2014, it was over 65%. Productive business lending — credit to firms outside the financial sector — shrank as a share across the same period.

## The instability that comes with it

When banks create money through lending, they also create a mirror-image debt. Every new deposit is someone's asset; every new loan is someone's liability. When the cycle runs up — rising asset prices enabling more lending enabling more price rises — the total debt load in the economy grows. When it reverses, the consequences are not symmetrical.

Falling asset prices reduce collateral values, which reduces new lending, which reduces deposits, which shrinks the money supply — precisely when the economy most needs liquidity. This is why banking crises produce deflationary spirals. The money supply contracting is not a metaphor; it is a mechanical consequence of loan books being written down and new credit drying up.

This is also why the standard Keynesian prescription of government stimulus has to work against a headwind in a financial crisis: the government is trying to inject money into an economy where the private banking system is simultaneously destroying it.

## What reform looks like

Proposals to restructure money creation range from incremental to fundamental. Stronger capital requirements make banks hold more of their own funds against losses, reducing the leverage amplifying the cycle. Sectoral lending guidance — used in some jurisdictions — can direct credit toward productive investment and away from pure asset speculation. Macroprudential tools like loan-to-value caps limit the degree to which rising prices self-perpetuate through credit.

More radical proposals include "full reserve" banking (requiring banks to hold 100% reserves against deposits, eliminating credit-based money creation) or sovereign money creation (returning money issuance entirely to the state). These remain outside mainstream policy but are debated seriously in monetary economics. The common thread in all reform proposals is acknowledging that money creation is an economic policy choice, not a natural process — and that leaving it entirely to commercial incentive has distributional consequences.
