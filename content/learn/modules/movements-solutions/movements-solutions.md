## What the history shows

The How Movements Change Systems path traced six eras of collective action. The pattern that emerges across all of them is consistent: movements that achieved durable change built institutional capacity alongside street presence, constructed broad coalitions that spanned veto players, and found or created political windows for their demands.

The failures show the inverse: movements that relied on visibility alone dissipated when attention moved on. Movements that won a single reform without building the capacity to defend it saw it reversed. Movements that excluded potential allies — on grounds of ideological purity or tactical disagreement — found themselves outflanked.

Digital movements added a new variable. The marginal cost of mobilisation has fallen to near zero, which means movements can scale very fast — but also means the barrier to counter-mobilisation has fallen equally. Fast scaling without institutional infrastructure produces spectacular visibility and fragile power.

## The strategic lessons

**Infrastructure precedes scale.** The labour movement's most durable achievements — the eight-hour day, collective bargaining rights, social insurance — came after decades of institution-building: unions, legal defence funds, political parties, worker education programmes. The NAACP's legal strategy required twenty years of infrastructure investment before producing Brown v. Board. The printing press enabled the Reformation not because it was a mobilising tool but because it allowed ideas to be transmitted faster than existing institutions could suppress them.

**Coalitions must span veto players.** Every significant reform has faced actors with the power to block it. Movements that engaged those actors — even partially — progressed further than those that maintained ideological distance. South Africa's anti-apartheid coalition included business interests. The US civil rights movement built relationships with religious institutions and labour unions that provided resources and legitimacy. This is not a moral argument for compromise — it is an empirical observation about what has worked.

**Pre-figurative institutions demonstrate viability.** Movements that created visible, functioning alternatives — the Mondragon cooperatives, the Vienna housing programme, free schools, community health centres — built political credibility alongside pressure. The institution proves the alternative works; the movement scales it.

**Timing matters more than rightness.** Reforms that seemed impossible became inevitable within years when the political window opened. The window is usually opened by a crisis, a scandal, or a shift in who holds legislative power. Movements that had done the preparatory work — research, coalition-building, draft legislation — were ready to move when the window opened. Those that had not watched the window close.

## What movements can do now

The institutional capacity that past movements built took decades. Starting that work now, in contexts where the political window may be some years away, is the historically consistent thing to do: building the coalitions, research capacity, legal infrastructure, and pre-figurative institutions that turn the next window into durable change.
