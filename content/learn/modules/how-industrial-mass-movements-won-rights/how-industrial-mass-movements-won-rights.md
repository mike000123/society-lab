## The conditions that made movements necessary

The Industrial Revolution concentrated workers in factories, mines, and mills under conditions determined almost entirely by employers and property owners. Working days of twelve to sixteen hours were standard in early British textile mills. Children as young as five worked in coal mines. Industrial injuries were common and uncompensated. There were no minimum wages, no limits on working hours, and no legal right to form organisations to bargain collectively.

These conditions were not natural or inevitable. They were outcomes of specific legal frameworks — master and servant laws that made breach of employment contract by workers a criminal offence, laws against trade union "combination," and property law that gave employers full control over the terms of engagement on their premises. The conditions were set by law, and changing them required changing law, which required political power workers did not yet have.

The early labour movement operated in this environment: organising was illegal, strikes could result in prosecution, and employer retaliation (blacklisting, eviction from tied housing, calling in police or private guards) faced no legal constraint. The movement grew despite — and partly because of — these conditions.

> A generation of workers in the 1830s and 1840s did not live to see the changes they organised for. They organised anyway. The logic: if you don't organise, nothing changes; if you do, you might leave something for those who follow.

## The eight-hour day: a three-continent campaign

The eight-hour day is the most internationally resonant demand in labour history, embodied in the slogan "Eight hours for work, eight hours for rest, eight hours for what we will." The campaign to achieve it spanned half a century and several continents.

In the US, the Federation of Organized Trades and Labor Unions (predecessor to the AFL) declared 1 May 1886 the deadline for the eight-hour day. The general strike that followed was the largest in American history to that point. In Chicago, a protest bomb at Haymarket Square killed police officers; eight labour organisers were tried and four executed in a trial widely considered a miscarriage of justice. The movement was set back, but not broken. May Day became the international workers' holiday precisely because of Haymarket.

In Australia, stonemasons in Melbourne won the eight-hour day in 1856 — the first workers in the world to do so legally. Their strategy was simpler: they stopped work at the end of eight hours and walked off the site. There was no pre-announced campaign; the action preceded any formal organisation.

The International Labour Organization (ILO), founded in 1919 after the First World War, adopted the eight-hour day as its first Convention. By this point, it had already been won in many countries through direct industrial action. The ILO's role was to codify and extend what movements had already extracted.

## How legal recognition changed the balance

Throughout the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, labour movements pushed not just for specific conditions but for the legal right to organise and bargain collectively. This was a different kind of demand: not for a particular wage or hour limit, but for the institutional framework within which future demands could be made.

The Wagner Act in the US (1935) is the clearest example. It established workers' legal right to organise and bargain collectively, prohibited employer interference with union elections, and created the National Labor Relations Board to enforce these rights. The Act was a product of the Depression-era political moment — the New Deal — but it was also the culmination of decades of labour movement pressure.

The effect was substantial. Union membership in the US rose from roughly 10% of the workforce in 1933 to nearly 35% by the mid-1950s. This period saw the largest sustained wage growth for working-class Americans in the country's history. The compression of income inequality between 1940 and 1980 is closely correlated with union density.

## The limits and reversals

The history of industrial movements is not a story of continuous progress. Gains were made, and gains were reversed. The period since 1980 in most OECD countries has seen declining union density, stagnating wages at the middle and bottom of the distribution, and the erosion of many of the legal protections won in the mid-twentieth century.

The political and legal environment shifted. Thatcher's anti-union legislation in the UK in the 1980s — secondary picketing bans, mandatory ballots, restrictions on closed shops — significantly weakened the institutional base of the labour movement. In the US, the decline of manufacturing, the spread of "right to work" laws in many states, and aggressive employer opposition to unionisation drove union density from 35% to under 10% in the private sector by the 2020s.

The question of what changes these reversals in turn — whether new organising models, political alliances, or economic crises create conditions for rebuilding collective power — is contested and ongoing.
