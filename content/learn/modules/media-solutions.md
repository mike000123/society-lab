## The architecture of manipulation

The Media, Attention & Digital Power path showed how the information environment works against citizens. Public relations professionals borrow the aesthetics of social movements and scientific authority to make commercial and political interests appear as grassroots concern. Outrage algorithms amplify division because division maximises engagement and engagement maximises ad revenue. Surveillance capitalism treats human experience as raw material for behavioural prediction products — the more granular the prediction, the more valuable the product.

The digital movement modules added a complication: the same infrastructure that enables manipulation also enables genuine organising. The question is whether the rules governing these platforms serve the people who use them or the advertisers who fund them.

## The reform agenda

**Regulate the advertising model.** The outrage economy is a direct consequence of behavioural advertising — platforms optimise for engagement because engagement drives ad revenue. Prohibiting micro-targeted advertising based on personal data profiles removes this incentive. The EU's GDPR enforcement, Norway's Consumer Authority orders, and Belgium's court rulings against Meta all show that regulatory action is legally viable. The Digital Services Act's requirement for non-profiling feed options is the next step: creating an affirmative obligation to offer alternatives, not just prohibiting specific harms.

**Break platform monopolies.** Network effects concentrate power in a handful of platforms. Interoperability mandates — requiring platforms to allow connection with third-party services — restore competition without requiring state-run alternatives. The EU Digital Markets Act's messaging interoperability requirement is the first major test of this approach.

**Fund alternatives.** The collapse of local journalism is not a market failure that competition will fix — it is a structural consequence of advertising migrating to platforms that do not fund journalism. Australia's News Media Bargaining Code, Norway's press subsidies, and the BBC's license-fee model represent different approaches to the same structural problem: news that serves the public interest needs funding that does not depend on maximising engagement.

**Build media literacy into the education system.** Finland's integration of source evaluation, narrative analysis, and platform mechanics into the core curriculum from age seven produces citizens who are measurably more resistant to manipulation. This is the fastest-acting lever available — it reaches younger cohorts who will shape public discourse for decades, and it can be implemented without confronting platform lobbying.
