## The three-institution system

The EU's legislative process is unusual among democracies because law-making power is genuinely shared between three institutions with different compositions and mandates.

The **European Commission** is the executive — 27 commissioners, one per member state, but formally independent of national governments. It has the sole right to initiate legislation. No MEP or member state can put a bill to a formal vote without a Commission proposal. This "right of initiative" gives the Commission enormous gatekeeping power over the EU's policy agenda.

The **European Parliament** is directly elected by EU citizens. It can amend and reject Commission proposals. Since the Lisbon Treaty (2009), it is a genuine co-legislator for most EU policy — not simply a consultative body. Its committees draft detailed positions with hundreds of amendments.

The **Council of the EU** (not the European Council — a common source of confusion) represents member state governments. Each country's relevant minister attends depending on the subject: finance ministers for economic law, environment ministers for climate regulation. The presidency rotates among member states every six months. For most legislation, a "qualified majority" of 55% of states representing 65% of the EU's population is enough to pass.

> The EU's design explicitly prevents any single institution from dominating. The cost is that everything is slow and every major reform requires sustained, multi-arena coalition building.

## How a law actually moves

A typical major EU regulation follows this path:

**Commission develops a proposal** — usually over months or years, with impact assessments, stakeholder consultations, and input from expert groups. This is where early lobbying is most effective: the first draft defines the terrain for everything that follows.

**Parliament and Council work in parallel** — committees in both institutions scrutinise the proposal and adopt their own positions. In Parliament, a lead committee drafts a report; hundreds of amendments are tabled; a final Parliament position is voted in plenary. In Council, technical working groups of national officials work through the text before ministers vote.

**Trilogue** — if Parliament and Council adopt different positions, informal negotiations between their representatives and the Commission produce a compromise text. These meetings are not public. Their outputs are pre-agreed texts that both institutions then formally adopt. The vast majority of legislation is now agreed in trilogue.

The whole process typically takes two to three years for major legislation. The Digital Services Act took about fourteen months after the Commission proposal — relatively fast. The Artificial Intelligence Act took over three years.

## Where power actually concentrates

In practice, several actors consistently shape EU outcomes more than their formal role suggests.

**Large member states** — Germany, France, and Poland together can block a qualified majority. Their governments' positions in Council, and their MEPs' committee roles in Parliament, give them structural advantages.

**The Commission's expert groups** — over 800 formally registered expert groups advise the Commission while it develops proposals. Industry is heavily represented. A 2017 study by Corporate Europe Observatory found that roughly 75% of participants in Commission expert groups represented corporate interests.

**The European People's Party** — consistently the largest group in Parliament since the first direct elections, the EPP's internal agreements on positions heavily influence which amendments pass and which rapporteurs are appointed to major files.

**Council unanimity requirements** — on tax, foreign policy, treaty change, and EU own resources, every member state has a veto. Small states can block outcomes on these issues, giving them leverage to extract concessions on other matters.
