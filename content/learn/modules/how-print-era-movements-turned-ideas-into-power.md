## What changed with moveable type

Johannes Gutenberg's printing press, developed around 1440, did not immediately change the world. For the first decades it primarily reproduced manuscripts the church had already approved. The change came when political and religious movements grasped the new medium's potential and used it deliberately.

Before print, ideas spread through sermons, manuscripts copied by scribes, and oral tradition. Each copy introduced variations. Reach was limited by travel time. A scholar in Wittenberg could not simultaneously address people in Hamburg, Frankfurt, and Cologne with the same words. After print, identical copies could be produced in the hundreds or thousands and distributed across a continent within weeks. This was not merely a change in speed — it was a change in what kinds of political organisation were possible.

The key insight that movements eventually discovered: print enables the creation of *publics* — large groups of people who share a common text, a common reference, a common sense of participating in the same conversation. This is the precondition for nationalism, for religious reform movements, for mass politics.

## The Reformation: the first print movement

Martin Luther posted his 95 Theses in October 1517. Within two weeks, copies had circulated across Germany. Within two months, they had been reprinted across Europe. Within a decade, Luther had produced over 400 publications; his works constituted roughly a third of all books sold in Germany. This was not spontaneous — Luther and his allies understood the medium and used it strategically.

The Catholic Church had faced heresy movements before: the Waldensians, the Hussites, Jan Hus himself. These were suppressed partly because they lacked the means to spread their ideas faster than the church could mobilise against them. Luther operated in a fundamentally different environment. By the time church authorities sought to suppress his ideas, they had already been read by hundreds of thousands of people — including the princes whose political support would protect Luther from the fate of Hus (burned at the Council of Constance in 1415).

> Luther himself recognised what was happening: "Printing is the ultimate gift of God, and the greatest of them." He did not mean this theologically. He meant it politically.

The Reformation restructured the relationship between individuals, their churches, and political authority — permanently. The translation of the Bible into vernacular languages, enabled by print, gave individuals direct access to the foundational text of their religion without clerical mediation. This was not merely a religious change; it was a challenge to the entire structure of intellectual and moral authority that the church embodied.

## Pamphlets, newspapers, and the emergence of mass politics

The same logic that enabled the Reformation enabled mass political movements two centuries later. The English Civil War (1640s) produced an extraordinary proliferation of pamphlets — thousands of titles addressing everything from constitutional theory to radical egalitarian proposals. The Levellers, the most radical faction, were notable users of print: their Agreement of the People (1647), a proposed written constitution with popular sovereignty and universal male suffrage, circulated through the New Model Army and reached a broader public than any previous constitutional proposal in English history.

The American Revolution was conducted partly as a print campaign. Thomas Paine's *Common Sense* (1776) sold 100,000 copies in its first three months — in a colonial population of 2.5 million, roughly half of whom were literate. The Federalist Papers and Anti-Federalist Papers were newspaper essays, written to persuade a public that was now understood as a political actor capable of being reasoned with.

The newspaper itself, which emerged as a mass medium in the eighteenth century, created the conditions for what Benedict Anderson called "imagined communities" — the sense of shared identity that underpins nationalism. Reading the same newspaper as thousands of strangers in the same morning created a shared temporal and informational world that was a precondition for national political identity.

## The contest over print

Governments and churches understood the threat quickly. The Index Librorum Prohibitorum (Index of Forbidden Books) was established by the Catholic Church in 1559. In England, the Licensing of the Press Act 1662 established pre-publication censorship. In France, the ancien régime operated an extensive system of permissions, privileges, and police surveillance of printers.

These were partially effective but ultimately inadequate. The technology of print was too widely distributed and the demand for it too strong. Underground printing, smuggled books, and the porous borders between jurisdictions meant that censorship could delay and obstruct but not prevent the spread of ideas.

The abolition of pre-publication censorship — in England in 1695, in the US through the First Amendment, in France by the Revolution — removed the formal bottleneck. What replaced it was not a free press in the idealistic sense but a press shaped by the economics of printing, the interests of printers and publishers, and the continuing contest over what could be said and by whom.
