## The measurement problem is also a governance problem

The Wellbeing Beyond GDP path traced how GDP became the dominant measure of social progress — and why that choice has consequences. GDP counts car accidents and prisons as growth but ignores unpaid care and community. It has no ecological ceiling. It misses distribution entirely: GDP per capita can rise while median wages fall. Doughnut Economics mapped the social foundations and ecological ceilings that a genuinely wellbeing-oriented economy must stay within. Cities and housing showed how the built environment shapes daily wellbeing through choices people do not consciously make. Wealth inequality showed how gains at the top mask stagnation for most.

These findings converge on a diagnosis: the economy is optimised for the wrong thing, and the instruments governments use to steer it measure the wrong outputs.

## The reform agenda

**Replace GDP at the top of the dashboard.** New Zealand's Wellbeing Budget and Wales's Future Generations Act both show what it means operationally to put wellbeing alongside fiscal balance as a headline target. The institutional mechanism matters: it is not enough to produce a supplementary report — wellbeing indicators need to be embedded in budget scrutiny, departmental targets, and judicial review of major public decisions.

**Redesign cities for wellbeing.** Cities determine daily stress, physical health, social connection, and ecological footprint more than most national policies. Investing in walkability, public space, mixed-use neighbourhoods, and affordable housing is not a lifestyle preference — it is evidence-based wellbeing policy. The Amsterdam Doughnut City initiative and Copenhagen's integration of doughnut indicators into urban planning show what embedding this looks like at city scale.

**Give people time and security.** The research on wellbeing consistently shows that after a moderate income threshold, time, autonomy, and social connection matter more than additional income. Four-day week trials and basic income pilots both show that reducing coerced labour time and economic precarity produces wellbeing gains that GDP growth cannot replicate.

## The political economy

Wellbeing reform is politically easier than it looks. The public consistently tells pollsters they want governments to prioritise health, security, and environment over growth. The resistance is primarily from business interests that benefit from the current measurement framework and from Treasury officials trained to optimise for GDP metrics.

Coalition-building that includes health systems (which bear the costs of poor wellbeing), local governments (which deliver most wellbeing-relevant services), and employers who benefit from healthier workforces can move faster than national fiscal politics alone.
