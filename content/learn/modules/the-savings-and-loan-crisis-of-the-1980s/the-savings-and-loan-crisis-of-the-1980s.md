## What savings and loan associations were

Savings and loan associations (S&Ls, also called thrifts) were a type of financial institution created specifically to channel household savings into home mortgages. For decades under the New Deal regulatory framework, they were tightly constrained: they paid limited interest on deposits, charged fixed rates on long-term mortgages, and were insured by federal deposit insurance.

This was a stable model in a stable interest-rate environment. The inflation and interest rate shocks of the late 1970s broke it. When the Federal Reserve raised interest rates sharply to fight inflation in 1979–1981, S&Ls faced a structural crisis: they held long-term mortgages at old low fixed rates while having to pay high current rates to attract depositors. Hundreds were technically insolvent.

The political response was deregulation. Starting with the Depository Institutions Deregulation and Monetary Control Act of 1980 and the Garn-St. Germain Act of 1982, S&Ls were allowed to pay competitive interest rates, expand into commercial real estate, corporate bonds, and other speculative assets — while retaining federal deposit insurance. This was the fundamental error.

> Deposit insurance removes the depositor's incentive to monitor risk. Deregulation removed the regulator's mandate to do so instead. The gap was filled by speculation.

## How the crisis developed

With deposit insurance protecting depositors and deregulation permitting high-risk lending, S&L owners and managers had a specific set of incentives: make risky loans and investments, collect fees and salaries, and wait to see if they paid off. If they did, profits were private. If they did not, the federal deposit insurance fund covered depositors' losses.

Several S&Ls engaged in straightforward fraud — rigged real estate appraisals, circular deals that generated paper profits and fees while adding nothing of value. Charles Keating's Lincoln Savings and Loan is the most famous example: by the time it failed in 1989, it had depleted $3.4 billion in depositor funds through complex deals, and 21,000 bondholders — many elderly — lost their savings.

Even without fraud, the structural incentive problem produced massive losses. Commercial real estate values in Texas and other oil-patch states collapsed in the mid-1980s. S&Ls that had loaded up on those loans failed in large numbers.

The regulatory system, hollowed out by the deregulatory ideology of the period, did not intervene. The FHLBB (Federal Home Loan Bank Board) was understaffed and politically pressured. The forbearance strategy — allowing failed institutions to continue operating in hope of recovery — allowed losses to accumulate for years before the reckoning came.

## What the cleanup revealed

The Resolution Trust Corporation (RTC), created in 1989, was responsible for managing and disposing of the assets of failed S&Ls. It eventually handled the assets of over 700 failed institutions. The total cost to taxpayers was approximately $130 billion — around $300 billion in 2024 dollars.

What the cleanup revealed was the systematic nature of what had occurred. This was not a run of bad luck. It was a predictable consequence of a specific institutional design: private owners with limited downside, unlimited upside, and insured depositors who had no reason to care about risk. The Resolution Trust sold assets at heavy discounts because markets had no appetite for the real estate and junk bonds that S&Ls had accumulated.

The Keating Five — five US senators who intervened with federal regulators on Keating's behalf — gave the crisis its clearest illustration of how political influence had compounded the deregulatory failure. The episode helped pass modest campaign finance reform and produced a renewed, brief interest in financial regulation that faded within a decade.
