## The scale and speed of decolonisation

In 1945, European empires controlled roughly a third of the world's land surface and a quarter of its population. By 1975, virtually all of it had achieved formal independence. This was an extraordinarily rapid political transformation, achieved within the lifetimes of single generations.

The speed was not accidental. The Second World War had simultaneously weakened European colonial powers — economically, militarily, and in terms of their claims to civilisational superiority — and activated colonial populations through military service, wartime propaganda about freedom, and the visible military defeats of white imperial powers by Japan. The war created the conditions; organised political movements translated those conditions into outcomes.

India's independence in 1947 was the largest single act of decolonisation — a country of 350 million people. It was achieved through three decades of Indian National Congress organising, culminating in the mass civil disobedience campaigns of the 1930s and 1940s. Gandhi's innovation was not principled non-violence per se but the strategic insight that British rule depended on Indian cooperation — with the law courts, tax collection, military service, and the civil service. Withdrawing that cooperation at scale made governing India more costly than withdrawing from it.

> The lesson Indian independence taught every subsequent anti-colonial movement: empires were not inevitable structures. They could be made ungovernable.

## Strategies across different contexts

Not all independence movements used non-violent civil disobedience. The strategic choice depended on the specific colonial situation.

In Kenya, the Mau Mau uprising (1952–1960) was a violent insurgency primarily among the Kikuyu people, responding to land seizures and racial exclusion. The British suppressed it with detention camps and brutal counter-insurgency — but the costs of suppression accelerated the political calculation that Kenya was not worth holding. Declassified records established that British forces detained over 150,000 people in conditions the High Court later characterised as systematic torture.

In Algeria, the National Liberation Front (FLN) fought an eight-year war against French rule that ended in 1962 independence. France had declared Algeria an integral part of the French Republic, not a colony — a legal distinction designed to make independence legally impossible. The FLN's strategy was to make the cost of holding Algeria politically intolerable in France itself, as well as militarily unwinnable in the field.

In Ghana, Kwame Nkrumah's "Positive Action" campaign of 1950 combined strikes, boycotts, and mass non-cooperation to force the British into negotiating independence, achieved in 1957 — the first sub-Saharan African state to gain it. Nkrumah coined the phrase "Seek ye first the political kingdom" to capture his conviction that formal political power was the prerequisite for economic transformation.

## Cold War leverage

The US was rhetorically anti-colonial — Roosevelt had pressed Churchill during the war to commit to Indian independence. More practically, the US saw newly independent states as a strategic arena in competition with the Soviet Union. This gave independence movements diplomatic cards to play that earlier generations lacked.

Several independence leaders — Ho Chi Minh, Julius Nyerere, Nkrumah — drew on socialist ideology and cultivated Soviet and Chinese relationships. Whether or not they were genuinely committed to communism, the credible threat of alignment with the Soviet bloc accelerated European withdrawal by raising the stakes of continued colonial resistance.

The United Nations, founded in 1945 with a strong anti-colonial majority among newly admitted members, provided another arena. General Assembly Resolution 1514 (1960, the Declaration on the Granting of Independence) passed 89-0, with nine abstentions — all colonial powers. It had no enforcement mechanism but provided legal and moral ammunition to independence movements.

## Formal independence and structural constraints

The achievement of formal political independence was real and significant. But it did not automatically transform the economic and institutional structures through which colonial extraction had operated.

Many African states inherited borders drawn at the 1884–85 Berlin Conference to match European administrative convenience rather than ethnic, linguistic, or economic geography. They inherited legal systems, currencies (often pegged to colonial currencies in arrangements like the CFA franc, controlled by France), debt obligations, and dependence on commodity exports to former colonial markets.

Kwame Nkrumah introduced the concept of "neo-colonialism" in 1965 to describe this condition: formal political independence without substantive economic sovereignty. The debate over its accuracy continues, but the structural constraints inherited from colonial rule remain a major variable in understanding divergent post-independence development trajectories.
