## The rewrites

The current architecture of the global monetary system did not emerge from negotiation or accident. It was built through specific US decisions that changed the rules for everyone.

**1933: Roosevelt bans gold ownership.** In the depths of the Depression, Franklin Roosevelt issued Executive Order 6102 requiring Americans to hand over their gold to the Federal Reserve at $20.67 per ounce. The following year, the Gold Reserve Act repriced gold at $35 per ounce — a 41% devaluation of the dollar relative to gold, and an effective default on any contractual obligation denominated in gold-backed dollars. It was presented as an emergency technical measure. It was a redistribution of wealth from creditors to debtors on an enormous scale.

**1944: Bretton Woods writes dollar supremacy into international law.** At the Bretton Woods conference, 44 nations agreed to peg their currencies to the US dollar, which alone would remain convertible to gold at $35/ounce. The US was the only country that emerged from World War II with its productive capacity intact and the world's largest gold reserve. The architecture reflected that power — but also locked it in. Every central bank now needed dollars as reserve assets. The IMF and World Bank were created to manage the system, and both were headquartered in Washington.

**1971: Nixon ends gold convertibility in a weekend.** By 1971, the US had been running persistent trade deficits and funding the Vietnam War through money creation. Other countries — notably France under de Gaulle — had begun converting their dollar reserves to gold, draining US gold stocks. On 15 August 1971, Nixon announced on television that the US was suspending dollar-to-gold convertibility, effective immediately. The Bretton Woods system that 44 nations had spent weeks building in 1944 was dissolved in a weekend address. No international consultation took place.

> Each of these rewrites transferred enormous wealth between groups, changed the rules for every other country in the world, and was presented as a technical adjustment rather than a political choice.

## What the rewrites created

Nixon's 1971 move was expected to weaken the dollar. Instead, it produced something unexpected: dollar dominance without the gold constraint. Because global commodity markets — especially oil, formalised through petrodollar agreements with Saudi Arabia in 1974 — were denominated in dollars, the world continued to need dollars regardless of the gold link. Central banks continued holding dollar reserves because there was no viable alternative.

This created the structure that persists today. The dollar accounts for roughly 58% of global foreign exchange reserves — down from about 70% in the 1990s but still larger than all other currencies combined. US Treasury bonds are the world's default safe asset. International borrowing is typically denominated in dollars. Countries need dollars to pay for oil, service external debt, and hold reserves — a demand that exists independent of any US trade surplus or fiscal virtue.

The result is what Valéry Giscard d'Estaing called the "exorbitant privilege": the US can borrow at lower rates than any other country, run persistent current account deficits without triggering the currency crises that would hit others, and — uniquely — can use access to its currency as a weapon.

## Sanctions as financial exclusion

The SWIFT interbank messaging network connects over 11,000 financial institutions in 200 countries. Dollar-clearing — the settlement of dollar transactions — runs through correspondent banking relationships regulated under US law. This means the US Treasury and its Office of Foreign Assets Control (OFAC) can cut any institution or country off from the global financial system.

Russia's exclusion from SWIFT in 2022 was the largest use of financial exclusion in history. Iran has operated under dollar sanctions in various forms since 1979. Venezuela, Cuba, North Korea, and entities in many other countries face OFAC restrictions. The practical effect is to make dollar-denominated trade impossible — and since most international trade is in dollars, this is an economic weapon available only to the issuer of the reserve currency.

The more the US uses this power, the stronger the incentive for others to build alternatives. China has expanded its Cross-Border Interbank Payment System (CIPS) and has been settling some oil trade in renminbi. The trajectory is toward slow diversification, but de-dollarisation at scale remains distant.

## How US monetary policy becomes everyone's problem

When the Federal Reserve raises interest rates, every country that borrowed in dollars faces higher debt costs. Capital flows back toward dollar assets, weakening emerging-market currencies. The dollar rises, making dollar-denominated commodity imports more expensive globally.

In 2022, the Fed raised rates sharply to combat post-pandemic inflation. Sri Lanka defaulted. Pakistan went to the IMF. Currencies fell across sub-Saharan Africa. These were not primarily caused by US rate decisions, but the rate rise amplified vulnerabilities that dollar dependence had created. The Fed has no mandate to consider global spillovers. No international institution has the authority to constrain it.

This asymmetry — the US sets rules for a system everyone participates in, but only the US decides what those rules are — is the direct legacy of 1933, 1944, and 1971. The rules were rewritten then. They can be rewritten again.
