## Why incremental approaches keep falling short

The Climate & Ecological Limits path identified the structural reasons why the gap between stated goals and actual outcomes persists. GDP growth and carbon emissions remain coupled because efficiency gains are routinely overtaken by volume increases — the rebound effect means that each unit of economic activity gets cleaner, but there are more units. Pollution accumulates through systems with long lags and non-linear tipping points: the damage is invisible until it is irreversible. Cities and urban systems are where most emissions and most ecological footprint is generated, yet urban planning remains siloed from climate policy. And GDP as the primary policy target actively encourages the activities that generate emissions.

These are not separate problems. They share a structural cause: economic activity is priced and measured in ways that treat ecological destruction as an externality rather than a cost.

## The reform agenda

**Make ecological costs visible in prices.** Carbon pricing with border adjustments is the most direct mechanism. British Columbia's revenue-neutral carbon tax, the EU's Emissions Trading System, and the EU Carbon Border Adjustment all demonstrate different aspects of what effective carbon pricing looks like. The distributional problem — carbon taxes are regressive without redistribution — is solved by per-capita dividends, which also make the policy politically durable by making low-income households net beneficiaries.

**Create binding legal frameworks with independent enforcement.** The UK Climate Change Act model — five-year legally binding carbon budgets assessed by an independent committee — has produced the most consistent long-term emissions reductions of any major economy. The independence of the enforcement body from annual political cycles is the critical design feature. Targets alone, without independent review and legal enforceability, consistently fail.

**Align land use and urban systems with ecological limits.** Cities determine the daily emissions and ecological footprint of most people in wealthy countries. Zoning reform that enables density reduces transport emissions and land consumption. The ecological ceiling dimension of the doughnut — biodiversity, land use, water cycles — requires treating ecosystems as having legal standing, not just as resources with economic value. New Zealand's Whanganui River and Ecuador's constitutional rights of nature show two routes toward legal protection of ecosystems.

**Phase out what cannot be reformed.** Fossil fuel subsidies of $7 trillion annually (IMF, 2023) actively undermine every other climate policy. Removing them while protecting low-income households through targeted transfers is the single largest fiscal lever available. Iran and Indonesia's subsidy reform experiences show it is politically feasible with the right design.
