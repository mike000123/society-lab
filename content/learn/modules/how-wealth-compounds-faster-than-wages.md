## Big picture
Many people are told that inequality mainly reflects effort, talent, or education. That matters at the margin, but this module is about a deeper mechanism. Once wealth is already unevenly distributed, the owners of large asset bases often receive returns that compound faster than wages grow. That means the system can widen inequality even if nobody changes jobs, works harder, or becomes suddenly more innovative.

This is why the distinction between income and wealth matters so much. Wages are usually tied to labor markets, bargaining power, contracts, and time. Wealth income can come from property, shares, bonds, businesses, rents, and inheritance. When that existing stock keeps earning more than ordinary work can catch up with, concentration becomes a structural outcome.

> The important educational move is to see concentration as a design pattern, not only as a story about a few rich individuals.

## What the evidence layer shows
The attached evidence pages help learners separate three questions that are often blurred together.

- Inequality data shows how income and wealth are distributed across societies and how that distribution changes over time.
- Work and employment data reminds us that having more people in jobs does not automatically solve the distribution problem if the ownership side of the economy remains extremely concentrated.
- Working-hours data helps expose another false promise: asking people to work ever more cannot close a compounding gap that is being driven by asset returns and inheritance.

The visual lesson is simple but powerful: effort and time are not enough to close a gap that is being widened by reinvested assets, inheritance, and preferential rules around capital.

## Why this keeps reproducing
Wealth concentration is reinforced by several channels at once. Capital gains can be lightly taxed. Housing can function both as shelter and as an appreciating asset. Inheritances can move large stocks across generations. Financial systems can reward those who already have collateral and investable capital. At the same time, labor power may weaken through precarious work, lower union density, and higher exposure to debt.

That does not mean all returns to capital are illegitimate. It means that when the institutional setting strongly favors existing asset holders, the long-run result is predictable: wealth grows by scale, and scale itself becomes power.

## What to ask instead
The policy question is not only how to raise wages. It is also how to change ownership dynamics, tax treatment, inheritance transmission, housing speculation, and the bargaining power of people who rely on labor income.

For Society Lab, the larger lesson is that inequality is not just a social outcome. It becomes a political outcome too, because concentrated wealth can buy influence, shape media, and help defend the rules that made the concentration possible in the first place.
