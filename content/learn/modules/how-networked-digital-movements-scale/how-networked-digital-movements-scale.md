## Big picture
Networked movements differ from many earlier movement waves because they often begin with rapid public witnessing. A video, image, or personal testimony can circulate at massive speed, turning isolated experience into collective awareness in hours rather than months.

That changes the politics of entry. People can recognize themselves in a shared frame, repost it, donate, talk publicly, or join a local action without waiting for a slow chain of formal organizations. In that sense, digital media lowers the cost of identification and first participation.

> The crucial teaching point is that communication speed and institutional speed are not the same thing.

## Why some digital movements endure and others stall
Virality helps with agenda setting, witness, recruitment, and symbolic scale. It does not automatically solve leadership, bargaining, legal drafting, coalition discipline, or post-moment strategy.

That is why some networked movements transform public language but struggle to lock in durable reform, while others manage to build a bridge from online witness to unions, campaigns, local groups, policy advocates, and courtroom or legislative work.

## What learners should watch for
When comparing digital movements, ask: what was the trigger event, how fast did the narrative spread, what organizations existed underneath, and what institutional channel could turn the surge into durable change?

Those questions help students avoid two bad simplifications at once: "online activism is fake" and "if it trends, it has already won." The truth is more interesting. Platforms change the opening move, but not the whole game.
