## The compound problem

The Inequality & Power path traced the mechanisms through which wealth and power reinforce each other. Wealth compounds faster than wages because returns on capital structurally exceed returns on labour — Thomas Piketty's central finding. Tax havens shelter accumulated wealth from redistribution. Lobbying converts economic power into regulatory and legislative advantage. Corruption diverts public resources to private benefit. Electoral rules that amplify wealth concentration in political voice create feedback loops: concentrated economic power produces concentrated political power, which produces policy that further concentrates economic power.

These mechanisms are mutually reinforcing. Tax havens shelter the wealth that lobbying protects. Lobbying shapes the electoral finance rules that make corruption sustainable. Corrupt institutions protect the tax havens. Addressing any one without the others produces limited results.

## The reform agenda

**Slow the compounding.** Wealth taxes, inheritance reform, and capital gains taxation all address wealth concentration directly. Norway's wealth tax, South Korea's high inheritance tax, and the historical US estate tax during its most progressive era all demonstrate that taxing accumulated wealth is politically and practically feasible. The design challenge is international coordination to prevent capital flight — which is why the OECD global minimum tax is the most important reform precedent of the past decade.

**Redistribute labour income at source.** Sectoral collective bargaining — setting wage floors across entire industries rather than firm by firm — removes wages from competition and raises the bottom without requiring individual negotiations. Germany and Scandinavia's co-determination and sectoral bargaining frameworks have produced the most equal wage distributions in the OECD for half a century. New Zealand's Fair Pay Agreements reintroduced the mechanism after a thirty-year absence. Worker board representation gives labour a structural voice in decisions about wages, investment, and executive pay.

**Break the political transmission.** Public campaign finance, mandatory lobbying disclosure, independent anti-corruption agencies, and electoral reform all address how economic power converts into political power. These reforms do not reverse inequality directly — but they break the feedback loop that allows economic concentration to perpetuate itself through the political system. Without them, tax and labour reforms are passed, weakened, and eventually reversed by the same concentrated interests they target.

## The coalition challenge

Inequality reform faces the most concentrated opposition of any reform agenda — the interests that benefit from the status quo have the most resources to defend it and the most direct access to the levers of political power. Historical changes in inequality — the mid-century compression in wealthy countries — came through combinations of war, mass labour mobilisation, and the credible threat of more radical alternatives. None of those conditions currently obtain.

The more realistic near-term path runs through international coordination (tax), regulatory enforcement (lobbying), and the kind of electoral reform that changes who enters politics and what is rational for them to support. The window for each of these opens at different times, for different reasons — and movements that have done the work will be ready.
