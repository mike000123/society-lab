## Money is a system of rules, not a natural phenomenon

The Modern Money path showed that money is created by banks when they issue loans, that wealth compounds structurally faster than wages, that tax havens drain public revenue through legal architecture, and that the US's unique position in the global monetary system gives it asymmetric power. None of these are natural laws — they are institutional choices, and they can be changed.

## The reform agenda

**Reclaim money creation as a public function.** Fractional-reserve banking means private banks create most of the money supply by issuing loans. This is pro-cyclical: banks lend more in booms and less in downturns, amplifying the cycle. Full reserve proposals (Sovereign Money, the Chicago Plan) would limit banks to lending only funds they hold, making money creation a public decision. This is contested — opponents argue it would freeze credit — but the pilots in Iceland and the Swiss Vollgeld referendum show it is entering mainstream policy debate.

**Close the offshore escape routes.** Country-by-country public reporting, beneficial ownership registers, and the OECD global minimum tax are the three pillars of international tax enforcement. Each is either implemented or in advanced stages of implementation. Together they close the primary mechanisms through which corporate and individual wealth escapes taxation. The political will required is primarily at the international level — domestic resistance from financial centres like Luxembourg and the Cayman Islands remains the main obstacle.

**Return monetary gains to citizens.** Sovereign wealth funds — funded by resource revenues, public investment returns, or seigniorage — can distribute the gains from money creation as citizen dividends rather than allowing them to accrue to asset-holders. Norway and Alaska demonstrate the feasibility at different scales. The design question is governance: how to insulate the fund from short-term political pressure while keeping it democratically accountable.

## What's blocking change

The financial sector is among the most powerful lobbying constituencies in every country where these reforms are most needed. The revolving door between regulators and regulated is well-documented. International coordination is required to prevent capital flight, which means reform progresses at the pace of the slowest major jurisdiction — unless the EU or US acts unilaterally and forces others to respond.

The OECD global minimum tax agreement showed that international coordination can happen. It required a decade of OECD work, the political shock of the 2008 crisis, and the COVID fiscal expansion to create the window. The same dynamics could open windows for further monetary reform.
