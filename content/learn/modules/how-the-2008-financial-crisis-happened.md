## The chain of events

The 2008 crisis emerged from a chain of failures that were individually recognisable but collectively ignored until the system collapsed.

**Step 1 — Mortgage origination** became detached from risk. In the traditional model, a bank that issued a mortgage held it on its books and bore the consequences if the borrower defaulted. By 2006, most US mortgages were immediately sold to investment banks to be packaged into securities. The originating bank was paid a fee regardless of loan quality. Incentives shifted from "will this borrower repay?" to "can we close this loan today?"

**Step 2 — Securitisation** converted pools of mortgages into tradeable bonds — mortgage-backed securities (MBS) and their derivatives, collateralised debt obligations (CDOs). Rating agencies — Moody's, S&P, Fitch — were paid by the banks issuing the securities to rate them. Complex models, fed by data from a period when house prices had only risen, assigned AAA ratings to instruments that contained subprime loans.

**Step 3 — Leverage** amplified every position. Investment banks, hedge funds, and structured investment vehicles borrowed 30, 40, even 50 times their equity to hold these securities. When prices fell even slightly, the equity was wiped out, triggering fire sales that pushed prices further down.

**Step 4 — Interconnection** meant that when Lehman Brothers filed for bankruptcy in September 2008, no one knew which institutions were exposed to which other institutions through derivatives contracts. Credit froze globally in a matter of days.

> The crisis did not happen because everyone miscalculated independently. It happened because everyone had an incentive not to look too hard at what the system was doing.

## The deregulation that made it possible

The conditions for 2008 were built over the previous two decades. The 1999 repeal of the Glass-Steagall Act (which had separated commercial and investment banking since 1933) allowed commercial banks to take investment banking risks. The Commodity Futures Modernization Act of 2000 explicitly excluded over-the-counter derivatives from regulatory oversight. Successive regulators adopted a philosophy — associated with Alan Greenspan and Robert Rubin — that sophisticated financial actors should be trusted to manage their own risk.

These were not inevitable policy choices. They were contested decisions made under intense financial industry lobbying, with the explicit rationale that financial innovation was creating value and spreading risk. The crisis showed that the risk had not been spread — it had been hidden and concentrated in ways the models could not see.

## The bailouts and what they revealed

When the crisis hit, governments stepped in with emergency measures of extraordinary scale — $700 billion in the US TARP programme, coordinated central bank interventions, nationalisation of banks in the UK, Ireland, and Iceland. The rationale was systemic: letting major institutions fail would destroy the credit system that ordinary firms and households depended on.

What the bailouts revealed was the hidden fiscal guarantee that the financial system had been operating under all along. Institutions that had privatised gains through leverage were now socialised for their losses. The homes of roughly 9 million Americans were foreclosed. Unemployment in the US rose from 4.6% to 10%. In Spain and Ireland, it exceeded 25%. The institutions whose risk-taking caused this received guarantees and cheap credit. The households whose loans had been misrepresented received little.

This moral hazard — the knowledge that systemic institutions will be rescued — has not been resolved by post-crisis reform. Capital ratios are higher. Stress tests exist. But the "too big to fail" problem is structurally intact.
