:::cards Quick map
Credit booms inflate asset prices | When banks expand lending, the new money flows into assets — property, stocks, commodities. Rising prices create collateral that supports more lending. The loop runs until it doesn't.
Leverage amplifies both gains and losses | A bank lending out $90 for every $10 in equity is highly profitable in good times. In bad times, a 10% fall in asset values wipes out all equity and makes the bank insolvent.
Crises spread through interconnection | Banks hold each other's debt, trade through shared clearing houses, and compete for the same depositors. A failure at one node spreads quickly.
Governments absorb private losses | Deposit insurance, central bank emergency lending, bailouts, and nationalisation mean that the cost of systemic failure lands on the public rather than investors.
:::

## The recurring pattern

Economic historians Carmen Reinhart and Kenneth Rogoff catalogued 800 years of financial crises in *This Time Is Different* (2009). Their central finding: each crisis is preceded by confident claims that this time the old rules do not apply — that financial innovation, new regulation, or changed fundamentals have permanently reduced risk. They always have applied.

The common structure is:

1. A period of credit expansion, often triggered by financial deregulation or a new financial instrument
2. Rising asset prices that appear to validate the expansion
3. Increased leverage across the financial system
4. A trigger — a rate rise, an external shock, a fraud revealed — that causes asset prices to fall
5. Forced selling to meet margin calls, which drives prices lower still
6. Solvency crises in institutions with high leverage
7. Government intervention to prevent full systemic collapse

The pattern is not hidden. It was described by Hyman Minsky in the 1970s, by Walter Bagehot in the 1870s, and by earlier observers before them. The knowledge exists. What makes repetition possible is not ignorance but the institutional and political economy of finance.

> The people who profit most from credit booms have every incentive to fund research and lobbying that argues this time is different. The people who bear the cost of the subsequent crash are diffuse, politically weak, and often do not know they are at risk until after the fact.

## Why memory fades

After a major crisis, the political will for stricter regulation is briefly strong. The period 1933–1980 in the US saw tight banking regulation — the Glass-Steagall separation of commercial and investment banking, leverage limits, deposit insurance with examination teeth — and no systemic banking crises. Memory of the Depression and the Second World War kept political constraints on finance broadly acceptable.

As that memory faded, so did the political will to maintain constraints. The deregulatory wave of the 1980s and 1990s dismantled many of the post-Depression safeguards on the grounds that they were no longer necessary. Within a generation, the 2008 crisis produced losses larger than the entire S&L crisis, the 1987 stock crash, and the LTCM failure combined.

The post-2008 reforms (Dodd-Frank in the US, Basel III internationally) tightened leverage limits and introduced stress testing. By 2018, parts of Dodd-Frank had been rolled back for smaller banks. In 2023, Silicon Valley Bank — which had been exempted from enhanced prudential standards — failed, requiring FDIC intervention and emergency backstops for uninsured depositors.

## The political economy of regulation

Banking crises repeat in part because financial regulation is subject to a specific political dynamic. The regulated industry is small, organised, and highly motivated to influence the rules. The public that bears systemic risk is large, diffuse, and generally not focused on bank capital requirements until they fail.

This creates regulatory capture: the process by which regulatory agencies come to serve the interests of the industries they regulate rather than the public. It does not require corruption. It can operate through the revolving door (regulators who expect future employment in finance), through the informational advantage of regulated firms, and through the gradual narrowing of what is considered technically feasible.

The result is that each generation of reform is followed by a generation of re-loosening, which sets up the conditions for the next crisis.
