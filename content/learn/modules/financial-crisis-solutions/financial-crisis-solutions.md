## Why the pattern repeats

The Why Financial Crises Repeat path traced the same arc across three crises: deregulation creates new instruments and risk-taking opportunities; boom conditions make risk-taking appear safe; profits are privatised; the system tips; losses are socialised through bailouts; deregulation resumes. Each cycle produces more sophisticated financial instruments and a slightly modified regulatory response — but the structural logic remains.

The consistency of the pattern points to structural causes rather than individual failures. Deposit insurance without adequate oversight creates moral hazard: banks have an incentive to take risk because gains accrue to shareholders while the floor is guaranteed by the public. Regulatory capture — the gradual colonisation of oversight bodies by the industries they regulate — means each generation of reform is slowly reversed. And the political economy of financial crises means that bailout recipients become the primary voices in the reform debate.

## The reform agenda

**Automatic circuit breakers, not regulatory discretion.** The most effective crisis-prevention tools are rules-based and automatic rather than dependent on regulatory judgment during a boom. Counter-cyclical capital buffers — which require banks to hold more capital as credit expands — were introduced under Basel III and have been activated in multiple jurisdictions. Dynamic provisioning rules in Spain built buffers before the 2008 crisis. The design principle is to remove the political economy problem of tightening during popular booms by making tightening automatic.

**Credible bail-in, not bailout.** If creditors believe they will be bailed out, they price risk incorrectly and actively encourage risk-taking at the firms they fund. Credible bail-in frameworks — where bondholders absorb losses before taxpayers — change this calculation at source. The EU's Bank Recovery and Resolution Directive and the Credit Suisse AT1 bond write-down in 2023 both demonstrated that bail-in is technically and legally feasible. The political will to enforce it against large institutions remains the binding constraint.

**Restore Glass-Steagall logic.** Separating retail deposits from speculative trading limits the contagion when investment bets fail. The UK's ring-fencing rules imposed separation on major banks by 2019. Reinstating this separation more broadly removes the most direct transmission mechanism between speculative losses and depositor funds.

**Public banking as a competitive anchor.** A public retail bank — whether postal savings or a state-owned institution — sets a floor on banking service quality and pricing that disciplines private bank behaviour. North Dakota's Bank of North Dakota has operated profitably since 1919, providing below-market rates to local businesses and agricultural borrowers throughout every private banking crisis.
