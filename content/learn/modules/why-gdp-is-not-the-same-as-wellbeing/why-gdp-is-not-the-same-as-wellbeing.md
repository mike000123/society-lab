## Big picture
GDP became powerful because governments needed one simple macro number. It tracks the value of goods and services sold in the market, which makes it useful for comparing output over time, judging tax capacity, and seeing whether an economy is expanding or contracting.

The problem starts when one production measure becomes a philosophy of success. GDP can rise because people are working longer hours, because housing costs are climbing, because families are paying for private fixes to problems that public systems did not prevent, or because disasters created expensive cleanup work. The number goes up, but the experience of life may not.

> The core teaching point is simple: a bigger money flow is not automatically a better society.

## What the evidence layer shows
The attached Our World in Data pages help users see the mismatch clearly when they are read together rather than one at a time.

- Happiness data shows that income matters, especially at low income levels, but life satisfaction is shaped by health, trust, relationships, stability, and public conditions as well.
- Working-hours comparisons show that rich countries do not all achieve prosperity through the same amount of paid work. Some combine shorter hours with high productivity and strong living standards.
- Poverty trends are important because they remind us not to swing to the opposite extreme. Material progress matters. The lesson is not that money is irrelevant, but that money alone is too narrow.

Seen together, the charts shift the lesson away from a false choice. The point is not “GDP is meaningless.” The point is that if we only track output, we miss the difference between a society that produces well and a society that lives well.

## Why the mismatch keeps happening
GDP is easy to summarize, easy to compare, and deeply built into statecraft, media, and financial commentary. Wellbeing is more distributed. It lives in many places at once: housing, health, transport, free time, education, safety, and the quality of social life.

That means the political system often ends up rewarding what is easiest to count rather than what is most important to protect. A rise in output gets treated as proof that the system is healthy, even when the same system is quietly producing burnout, expensive housing, pollution, and fragility.

## What to ask instead
The better question is not whether GDP should disappear. The better question is what dashboard should sit next to it. A serious public scorecard would track affordability, time pressure, health, inequality, ecological damage, and whether ordinary people feel more secure and more free in their daily lives.

For learners, this module is a first step in systems thinking. It shows how a useful indicator becomes dangerous when it is asked to answer a question it was never designed to answer.
