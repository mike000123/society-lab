## The time-horizon mismatch

The mismatch is simple to describe but hard to solve. A politician seeking re-election has a rational planning horizon roughly equal to the electoral cycle — four or five years. A problem like climate change, pension system sustainability, or infrastructure maintenance requires sustained action over twenty to fifty years, with costs front-loaded and benefits deferred.

The result is not ignorance. Finance ministries, the IMF, the OECD, and national research bodies have published detailed projections of pension gaps, infrastructure backlogs, and climate commitments for decades. Governments know. The problem is that acting on that knowledge is a losing electoral trade: you bear the political cost of unpopular taxes, cuts, or constraints today; someone else will be in office to claim credit when the problem doesn't materialise.

This is why long-term problems tend to be acted on only when they become acute — when the pension fund is nearly exhausted, when the bridge collapses, when the climate effects become visible and costly enough to produce immediate electoral pressure. At that point, the cost of action is far higher than it would have been if addressed early.

> The political calendar and the policy calendar are misaligned. Most of what governments know they should do, they cannot afford to do before the next election.

## The discount rate problem

Economics describes this with the concept of a discount rate: how much less is a benefit worth if it arrives in the future rather than today? Private investors apply high discount rates — a pound in ten years is worth much less than a pound today. Elected politicians apply even higher implicit discount rates, because a political benefit in ten years is worth almost nothing to someone who needs votes in four.

This creates a systematic bias in public investment. Projects that produce visible ribbon-cuttings before the next election are preferred over maintenance that is invisible until failure. Tax cuts that put money in voters' pockets today are preferred over structural reforms that will improve fiscal sustainability in twenty years. Borrowing to fund current spending is preferred over saving for demographic transitions that are decades away.

The political business cycle — governments stimulating the economy before elections and consolidating after — is one of the most robust findings in political economics. It is not a conspiracy; it is the rational response to the incentive structure.

## Who doesn't have a vote

The most structurally excluded group in this problem is people who do not yet exist, or who are too young to vote. The decisions a government makes today on climate, public debt, infrastructure, and education shape the world those people will inherit — but they have no formal voice in the system making those decisions.

Those currently under eighteen will live with the consequences of climate commitments made in the 2020s well into the twenty-second century. People not yet born will inherit the debt accumulated to fund current consumption. They are the group most affected by intergenerational trade-offs and the group with no electoral representation at all.

Several countries have experimented with institutions to give future generations some form of representation: Wales has a Future Generations Commissioner with a statutory duty to consider long-term wellbeing in public decisions. Hungary had a Parliamentary Commissioner for Future Generations from 2008 to 2012. Finland's Committee for the Future is a standing parliamentary committee with a mandate to assess emerging issues over a 10-30 year horizon. These are partial measures — they have advisory rather than binding power — but they represent an attempt to insert a longer time horizon into a system built around the short one.

## The institutional buffering problem

The main existing response to electoral short-termism is delegation: move certain decisions to bodies insulated from electoral pressure. Independent central banks, independent fiscal institutions (parliamentary budget offices, fiscal councils), constitutional debt brakes, and administrative agencies with long terms are all versions of this.

The logic is clear: if you cannot trust politicians to make long-term decisions because their incentives are short-term, remove certain decisions from politicians. The ECB sets interest rates. The UK Office for Budget Responsibility publishes fiscal forecasts. Germany's constitutional Schuldenbremse caps structural deficits.

But each of these carries a cost: democratic accountability is reduced. The ECB is not elected and cannot be voted out. The OBR's forecasts can be overridden. The Schuldenbremse created inflexibility precisely when Germany needed to invest in infrastructure and energy transition — it was suspended in 2020 for COVID and again debated extensively in 2023-24. Independent institutions buffer short-termism but don't eliminate it, and they generate their own legitimacy problems when their decisions diverge from democratic preferences.
