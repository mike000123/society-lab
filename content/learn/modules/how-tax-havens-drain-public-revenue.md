## How the system works

Tax havens are not just small islands with palm trees and secrecy laws. The network is global and includes major financial centres — the Netherlands, Luxembourg, Ireland, Singapore, and parts of the United Kingdom — that offer specific combinations of low rates, favourable treaty networks, and light regulation to multinational firms.

The core mechanism is **profit shifting**: moving profits from where economic activity actually takes place to jurisdictions where the tax rate is low or zero, using entirely legal accounting methods. The most common techniques include:

**Transfer pricing** — a company's subsidiaries trade with each other at prices that can be set internally. A manufacturing subsidiary in a high-tax country buys components from a sister company in a tax haven at inflated prices, transferring profit to the low-tax entity. The OECD "arm's length" standard is supposed to prevent this, but it is difficult to enforce for intangible assets like patents and brands.

**IP holding structures** — a company's valuable intellectual property (a brand, a drug patent, a software licence) is held by a subsidiary in Ireland, Luxembourg, or the Netherlands. Operating companies in every other country then pay royalties to use that IP. The royalties are a cost in high-tax countries and income in low-tax ones.

**Treaty shopping** — using intermediate holding companies in countries with favourable double-tax treaties to reduce withholding taxes on dividends, interest, and royalties flowing across borders.

> The tax haven system is not about hiding money under a mattress. It is about routing profit through a chain of legal entities until it lands where it is taxed least.

## The revenue cost

Estimating the exact cost of corporate tax avoidance is difficult because the data is constructed to obscure it. But credible estimates put the figure at $500–$600 billion lost per year globally from corporate profit shifting alone, based on the gap between where economic activity occurs and where profits are reported.

The Tax Justice Network estimates that $427 billion is lost annually through corporate tax abuse and offshore wealth — roughly equivalent to the entire healthcare budget of the United Kingdom. Low- and middle-income countries lose a disproportionate share relative to their GDP, because they have less capacity to write and enforce complex anti-avoidance rules and less leverage in negotiating with multinationals.

Apple's tax structures through Ireland, Google's "Double Irish Dutch Sandwich," Amazon's Luxembourg arrangements, and Starbucks's Dutch IP holding company became well-known cases when investigated by the European Commission and US Senate — but they illustrate standard industry practice, not outliers.

## Why multilateral reform is essential and difficult

A single country cannot solve tax havens unilaterally. If Germany raises its corporate tax rate aggressively, multinationals can shift more profit to Ireland or Singapore. The country most likely to lose from unilateral action is not the tax haven but the country trying to reform.

The OECD's Base Erosion and Profit Shifting (BEPS) project, launched after the 2008 crisis, resulted in the 2021 global minimum corporate tax agreement — a landmark deal signed by over 130 countries committing to a minimum effective rate of 15% on large multinationals. This is a significant step. Critics note that 15% is well below the rates most countries charge domestic businesses, and that implementation loopholes and carve-outs have significantly diluted the agreement's effect.

The political economy of reform is difficult because some small economies — Ireland, Luxembourg, Cayman Islands — depend heavily on their haven status for employment and revenue. They have seats at international tables and strong incentives to slow reform.
